# 5. Idea 1 — Quality + Uptrend

## The compounding business, bought with the trend

The most fundamental techno-funda idea: own **high-quality businesses** — the compounding machines — but only while they are in an **uptrend**. This is the classic "great company at a reasonable time" construction.

## The idea

A quality business (high, sustained returns on capital) tends to compound in value over years. But even a great company goes through long periods where the stock *falls* or *goes nowhere* — and buying during those periods is painful, even if the eventual outcome is fine. The Quality + Uptrend idea simply waits: **identify the quality business, then own it only while price is trending up.**

The logic is that an uptrend is the market's own confirmation that the business's compounding is currently being *rewarded*. You are buying quality *and* paying attention to when the market agrees with you.

## The fundamental screen (what)

Over a liquid universe (the NIFTY 500 is a natural start):

- **ROE > 15%**, averaged over the last 5 years (sustained, not a one-year spike).
- **ROCE > 15%** (or a stable, high operating margin).
- **Low debt** — debt-to-equity < 0.5 for non-financials; interest coverage > 4×.
- **Consistent profitability** — positive earnings in nearly all of the last 10 years.

This produces a shortlist of genuine compounding businesses — often the "franchise" names with durable competitive advantages.

## The technical trigger (when)

From that shortlist, act only when:

1. **Price is above the rising 200-day MA** (uptrend confirmed).
2. **Entry trigger:** one of the timing signals from Chapter 4 — most naturally a **pullback to the 50-day or 20-day MA that holds and reverses**, or a clean **breakout to a new high**.
3. **Exit:** close below the 200-day MA (trend broken), or a stop below the pullback low.

## A worked example (an NSE franchise)

- A consumer staple with a 5-year average ROE of 24%, minimal debt, and consistent earnings passes the quality screen.
- For months it trades sideways, below its 200-day MA — *not* a candidate yet, despite the quality.
- A recovery begins; the stock **closes above its rising 200-day MA**, then **pulls back to the 50-day MA and reverses upward**. **Entry**.
- The stock trends higher for a year as earnings keep compounding; the investor holds while the 200-day MA keeps rising.
- A market correction drives it **below the 200-day MA**. **Exit** — the trend, and therefore the timing, has failed (the *business* may still be fine).

Result: the investor owned a great business *only during the periods the market was rewarding it*, and stepped aside when it was not.

## Risks and limitations

- **Late entries / late exits.** Waiting for the 200-day MA means giving up the first and last legs of every move. This is the price of confirmation.
- **Whipsaw in ranges.** A quality stock can chop around its 200-day MA, generating false entries/exits. The pullback trigger (rather than the raw MA cross) reduces this.
- **Quality is backward-looking.** A business that *was* high-ROE can deteriorate; the screen lags reality. The exit rule partly covers this, but the fundamental thesis must still be monitored.
- **Excludes great turnarounds.** The idea deliberately skips companies not yet in an uptrend — including some that will become the next big winners.

## Summary

- Own high-quality businesses (sustained ROE/ROCE, low debt) only in uptrends.
- Enter on a pullback reversal or breakout above the rising 200-day MA; exit on a trend break.
- The uptrend is the market's confirmation that quality is being rewarded.
- Costs: lateness, whipsaw, and excluding turnarounds.
