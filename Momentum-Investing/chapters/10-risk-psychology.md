# 10. Risk and Psychology for the Long-Term Investor

## The hardest part of momentum is patience

The strategies in this book are simple to state and genuinely hard to *hold*. The difficulty is not intellectual; it is psychological — because the momentum premium arrives in **lumps**, with long stretches of underperformance in between. This chapter names the risks and the discipline required to stay the course.

## The two big risks

### 1. The momentum crash

Momentum portfolios hold recent winners. After a sharp market *bottom*, the prior **losers** recover fastest, and the portfolio — positioned in the prior winners — can lag the market dramatically for a period. This is not a hypothetical tail; it is the *known, recurring* failure mode of momentum.

The defence is structural, not psychological:

- **Long-only** momentum (hold winners, hold cash/benchmark — not shorting losers) avoids the worst of the crash.
- The **absolute filter** (200-day MA) steps the portfolio aside in sustained downtrends.
- **Diversification** across names/sectors limits any single reversal's damage.

Even so, the investor must accept that momentum *will* occasionally and sharply underperform — and that this is the premium's price, not proof the strategy is broken.

### 2. The discipline to hold through underperformance

The second risk is subtler: **quitting.** Momentum underperforms the broad index for stretches — sometimes a year or more. During those stretches, it *looks* broken, and the investor is tempted to abandon it — usually at the exact bottom, right before the factor rebounds. This "strategy abandonment at the worst moment" is the single most common way momentum investors destroy their own returns.

## The psychology of holding

The specific psychological traps, and their defences:

### Trap 1: confusing a drawdown with a broken strategy

A momentum drawdown *feels* like evidence the strategy no longer works. But a drawdown is the *expected* behaviour of a strategy whose returns come in lumps. **Defence:** decide your evaluation horizon *in advance* — momentum is judged over 5–10 years, not quarters — and refuse to re-evaluate mid-drawdown.

### Trap 2: loss aversion in the rebalance

The rebalance will occasionally force you to sell a loser (painful) and buy something that just ran up (feels like chasing). **Defence:** the rebalance is mechanical precisely *because* the feelings will be wrong at the moment of decision. Follow the calendar.

### Trap 3: chasing recency

When momentum is working, it is tempting to concentrate harder, add leverage, or trade more. When it is lagging, it is tempting to "wait for confirmation" and sit out. Both are recency bias. **Defence:** the allocation and schedule are fixed; change them only through a pre-committed review process, never reactively.

### Trap 4: the itch to intervene

A patient, rules-based strategy is boring. The boredom invites "improvements" — tweaks that are really un-tested deviations. **Defence:** any change must be tested over the same long horizon the strategy itself is judged on; otherwise, it is noise.

## The practices that keep you in the game

1. **Write the rules down.** A one-page system document — universe, lookback, selection, rebalance schedule, absolute filter — is a contract with your future self.
2. **Automate what you can.** A calendar, a checklist, or a systematic rebalance removes the in-the-moment decision.
3. **Measure in decades.** Momentum is a multi-decade factor. Judge it on the horizon it was designed for.
4. **Separate the process from the outcome.** A losing year that followed the rules is a process success; a winning year that broke them is a future disaster.
5. **Accept the premium's price.** The drawdown *is* the fee for the excess return. An investor who cannot pay it will not collect the return.

## The honest bottom line

Momentum investing is not hard because it is complicated — it is hard because it is **simple and uncomfortable**. The factor is real, the rules are easy, and the entire battle is one of *staying the course* through the periods when the strategy looks wrong. The investor who can follow the calendar, honour the filter, and hold through the drawdowns has already done the hard part. Everything else is arithmetic.

## Summary

- Two big risks: the momentum crash, and quitting during underperformance.
- The crash is defended structurally (long-only, absolute filter, diversification).
- Quitting is defended psychologically (fixed horizon, written rules, automation).
- The rebalance is mechanical because the feelings will be wrong at decision time.
- The premium's price is the drawdown; pay it, or don't collect the return.
