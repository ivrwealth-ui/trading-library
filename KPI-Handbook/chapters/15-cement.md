# 15. Cement

**At a glance.** Cement is the classic "low-value, high-volume" commodity — heavy, perishable (~90-day shelf life), and expensive to transport, so the industry competes regionally rather than nationally. The value chain is: limestone quarry → clinker plant → grinding unit → market. Almost all profit levers are *cost* levers. Listed leaders include UltraTech, Ambuja, ACC, Shree Cement, and Dalmia Bharat.

## The KPIs

**Installed capacity and capacity utilization** — production capacity and the share actually used.

*Why it matters:* Cement is capital-intensive; utilization drives operating leverage. Adding capacity into a demand lull compresses utilization and prices.

*How to read:* 70–80% utilization is typical. A firm growing capacity *while* holding utilization is growing into demand; a firm adding capacity with falling utilization is creating oversupply.

*Source:* Quarterly investor presentation and CMA (https://www.cmaindia.org).

**Sales volume (million tonnes)** — cement sold.

*Why it matters:* Volume is the demand side. Some cement makers even sell more than they produce (buying from others) to hold market share.

*How to read:* Compare volume growth with the industry growth rate. Consistent outperformance = market-share gain.

*Source:* Quarterly presentation.

**Realization per tonne (₹/MT)** — average selling price per tonne.

*Why it matters:* Realization reflects regional pricing and the trade/non-trade mix. Higher realization means the firm commands a price premium or serves better markets.

*How to read:* Compare realization across peers; differences reveal brand/pricing power and regional exposure. Watch realization *with* cost per tonne to get the margin.

*Source:* Quarterly presentation.

**Cost per tonne — input, power & fuel, and freight** — the three big cost centres as a ratio of revenue.

*Why it matters:* This is the heart of cement analysis. Input (limestone + raw materials), power & fuel (coal/petcoke + captive power), and freight (the biggest — cement is heavy) are the three levers a company controls.

*How to read:* Compute each as a % of revenue and compare across peers. The lowest freight ratio usually wins (grinding units close to market, captive fleets). A falling cost ratio = improving competitiveness.

*Source:* Annual report (break-up of expenses) and quarterly presentation.

**Clinker factor** — the proportion of clinker in the cement recipe.

*Why it matters:* Clinker is the most fuel-intensive step; a lower clinker factor (more fly-ash/slag blended in) cuts fuel cost *and* carbon footprint.

*How to read:* Lower is better, provided strength is maintained. Watch the clinker-to-cement ratio trend.

*Source:* Annual report and sustainability disclosures.

**Limestone reserves / mine life** — the years of limestone reserves remaining.

*Why it matters:* Limestone is the irreplaceable input, and captive reserves are the ultimate moat — without them, a plant depends on costly purchased limestone or is terminal.

*How to read:* Longer mine life (decades) is better. Watch reserve additions vs consumption.

*Source:* Annual report and MD&A.

**Lead distance / regional footprint** — how far cement travels from plant to market, and where the company operates.

*Why it matters:* Freight is the largest controllable cost, and cement is perishable, so proximity to market is everything. Regional diversification also hedges local demand/price swings.

*How to read:* Shorter lead distance = lower freight cost. A diversified regional footprint reduces single-market risk. Watch the trade vs non-trade (B2B) mix, too.

*Source:* Annual report and quarterly presentation.

## Healthy profile vs red flags

- **Healthy:** High utilization, volume growth above industry, realization holding, falling cost ratios, captive limestone reserves.
- **Red flags:** Aggressive capacity additions into a downcycle, freight ratio rising, dependence on purchased limestone, or realization falling faster than costs.
