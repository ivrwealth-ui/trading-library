# 20. Construction and Infrastructure (EPC)

**At a glance.** Engineering, Procurement and Construction (EPC) companies build the roads, bridges, metros, ports and buildings of the economy. The business is lumpy, debt-heavy and working-capital-intensive, driven by government infrastructure spending. Listed leaders include L&T, IRB Infrastructure, KEC International, NCC, PNC Infratech, and Dilip Buildcon (with peers in roads via asset-heavy HAM models).

## The KPIs

**Order book and order inflows** — backlog and new wins (same as capital goods, but even more central here).

*Why it matters:* For an EPC company, the order book is everything — it is the visible future revenue and the base for everything else.

*How to read:* Watch book-to-bill (>1 = growing backlog) and the *composition* (roads vs buildings vs metros vs water). Government budget cycles drive inflows.

*Source:* Quarterly results.

**Project mix — HAM vs EPC vs BOT** — the contract model split.

*Why it matters:* The model determines risk. Pure EPC is asset-light (build, get paid); Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM) adds a smaller equity stake and annuity payments; Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) is asset-heavy and traffic-risk-exposed.

*How to read:* A shift toward asset-light EPC reduces balance-sheet risk; a heavy BOT/HAM portfolio ties up capital and leverage. The trade-off is that asset-light firms give up the long-run annuity or toll income that asset-heavy ones keep.

*Source:* Annual report and investor presentations.

**Working capital and receivable days** — cash tied up in projects and unpaid bills.

*Why it matters:* EPC is chronically working-capital-hungry — clients (often government) pay slowly. Receivable and unbilled-revenue days are the health of the cash cycle.

*How to read:* Rising receivable days or growing unbilled revenue (work done but not yet billable) is the classic early warning of stress in EPC.

*Source:* **Screener.in** and annual report.

**Debt / leverage** — the debt burden.

*Why it matters:* EPC companies borrow to fund working capital and equity commitments; over-leveraging has broken many EPC firms in downcycles.

*How to read:* Watch net debt and interest coverage. A rising debt load against a flat order book is a red flag. Asset-light models should keep leverage low.

*Source:* **Screener.in** and annual report.

**Execution run-rate** — the pace at which the backlog is being converted to revenue.

*Why it matters:* Order books are only valuable if they execute. Slow execution delays revenue and, in HAM/BOT, delays the return of capital.

*How to read:* Compare quarterly revenue to the order book (implied execution period). A lengthening execution cycle flags project delays.

*Source:* Quarterly results.

**Margin and arbitration/slow-moving orders** — profitability and the quality of the backlog.

*Why it matters:* Old, slow-moving or disputed orders (arbitration, stalled land acquisition) sit in the book but don't convert to cash — they inflate the book and hide weak real demand.

*How to read:* Discount the slow-moving portion of the book. A book with a high share of old/stalled orders is lower quality than it looks.

*Source:* Annual report (ageing of order book) and concall.

## Healthy profile vs red flags

- **Healthy:** Growing book with a book-to-bill above 1, asset-light mix, disciplined receivables, low leverage.
- **Red flags:** Growing receivables and unbilled revenue, rising debt, slow-moving/stalled orders, or an order book concentrated in one state/sector.
