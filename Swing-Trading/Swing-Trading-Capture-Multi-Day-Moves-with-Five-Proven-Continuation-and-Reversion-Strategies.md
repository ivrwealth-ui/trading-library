# 1. What Is Swing Trading?

## The middle timeframe

**Swing trading** captures moves that unfold over **a few days to a few weeks** — longer than a day trade, shorter than an investment. The "swing" is a single leg of price movement: an upswing, a downswing, or a reversal between the two. The swing trader's job is to enter near the start of a swing and exit near its end.

It occupies a deliberate middle ground:

| Style | Holding period | Trades per month (rough) | Core skill |
|-------|----------------|--------------------------|------------|
| Day trading | Minutes to hours | Many | Speed, execution |
| **Swing trading** | **Days to weeks** | **A handful** | **Trade selection, patience** |
| Momentum trading | Weeks to months | Few | Trend following |
| Investing | Months to years | Very few | Patience, factor discipline |

## The two engines of swing trading

Swing trading is unique in that it deliberately harnesses **both** of the market's two great tendencies:

1. **Momentum / continuation.** In a trending market, a pullback is usually a *pause*, not a reversal — and the trend resumes. Swing strategies that buy pullbacks (Chapter 4) and breakouts (Chapters 5–6) exploit this.
2. **Mean reversion.** At extremes, prices tend to snap back toward the average. In a *ranging* market, or after a sharp overextension, swing strategies that fade the move (Chapters 7–8) exploit this.

The single most important skill in swing trading is therefore **knowing which regime you are in** — trending or ranging — and choosing the matching strategy. A continuation strategy traded in a range whipsaws; a reversion strategy traded in a trend gets run over. Chapter 3 is devoted to making this call.

## The anatomy of a swing trade

Every swing trade, whatever its engine, has the same four parts:

1. **Setup** — a defined condition that says "a swing may start here" (a pullback to support, a breakout, an oversold extreme).
2. **Entry** — a trigger that confirms the swing has begun (a reversal candle, a close above a level).
3. **Stop** — a price that, if reached, proves the setup wrong (below the pullback low, below the breakout level, beyond the extreme).
4. **Target** — a price where the swing is likely to exhaust (a prior high, the opposite end of the range, a measured move).

Unlike pure momentum trading — which lets winners run without a target — swing trading *usually* takes a profit at a target, because a swing is by definition a *bounded* move. This "defined risk, defined target" structure is what makes swing trading approachable and repeatable.

## Why swing trading suits many people

- **It is not a second job.** A handful of trades a month, with entries and exits set on the daily timeframe, fits around a normal life. You are not glued to the screen.
- **It uses defined risk.** Every trade has a stop, so the worst case is known *before* entry.
- **It works on daily charts.** Daily candles are less noisy than intraday, so the setups are more reliable and the decisions less frantic.
- **It is versatile.** It can be applied to indices (NIFTY, BankNIFTY), large-cap stocks, and even sectors — anything liquid with clean daily swings.

## What swing trading is not

- It is **not** day trading — you hold overnight, so you must be comfortable with *gap risk* (price opening far from where it closed).
- It is **not** a guarantee — swing setups fail often; the edge is in the *risk-reward ratio* (cutting losses fast, taking profits at targets), not in being right most of the time.
- It is **not** prediction — a swing trader reacts to *structure* (levels, trend, extremes), not to forecasts about where the market "should" go.

## Summary

- Swing trading captures multi-day to multi-week moves — the middle timeframe.
- It harnesses both momentum (continuation) and mean reversion, matched to the market's regime.
- Every trade has four parts: setup, entry, stop, target — defined risk, defined reward.
- It suits people who want active trading without being glued to the screen.
- It carries gap risk and requires discipline, not prediction.

# 2. The Swing Trader's Toolkit

## A small, sharp toolset

Swing trading rewards a *few* tools used well over a *wall* of indicators. Every strategy in this book uses only the following. Learn these once, and each strategy chapter becomes a matter of *combination*, not *definition*.

## Price levels: support and resistance

**Support** is a price level where buying has historically overwhelmed selling — a floor. **Resistance** is a level where selling has overwhelmed buying — a ceiling. They are the most fundamental concept in swing trading, because they define *where swings start and end*:

- A swing **up** often starts at support and ends at resistance.
- A swing **down** often starts at resistance and ends at support.

Key facts about levels:

- **Levels flip roles.** Broken resistance often becomes support (and vice versa) — the basis of the "breakout and retest" trade (Chapter 5).
- **Levels are zones, not lines.** A "level" is really a *zone* of a few points/percent, because the market rarely respects a single exact price.
- **The more times a level is tested, the more meaningful it is** — but also the more likely it is to eventually break.

## Moving averages: dynamic support and trend

A **moving average** (the average of the last N closes) acts as *dynamic* support or resistance, rising and falling with the trend. In swing trading the common uses are:

- The **20-period EMA** as dynamic support in an uptrend (the pullback magnet in Chapter 4).
- The **50- and 200-day MAs** as the trend context — above = uptrend, below = downtrend.

An average that is **sloping** is meaningful (it shows trend); a flat average is just noise.

## Oscillators: measuring the extreme

**Oscillators** measure whether price is stretched to one side, and are the engine of mean-reversion swings:

- **RSI (Relative Strength Index)** — bounded 0–100; values above ~70 are "overbought," below ~30 "oversold." Crucially, *overbought/oversold only means "likely to pause" in a range* — in a strong trend, RSI can stay extreme for a long time.
- **RSI-2** — a short, 2-period RSI used in the specific mean-reversion strategy in Chapter 7 (documented by Connors & Alvarez).

The discipline with oscillators: **use them in ranges and at extremes, not blindly in trends.** An overbought reading in an uptrend is often just *strength*, not a sell signal.

## Volume: confirmation

**Volume** confirms a move. A breakout on rising volume is more trustworthy than one on thin volume; a reversal off support is more meaningful when buyers step in with size. Volume is rarely a standalone signal in swing trading, but it is a useful *filter*: the best setups are confirmed by participation.

## Candlesticks: the entry trigger

**Candlesticks** (open, high, low, close per period) provide the *trigger* — the moment a setup becomes a trade. The patterns a swing trader cares about are few:

- **Reversal candles** — a candle that shows the prior move losing momentum: a long lower shadow off support (buyers rejected the lows), an engulfing candle, a hammer/doji.
- **Continuation confirmation** — a strong-bodied candle closing through a level.

The candlestick is the *finger on the trigger*; the level, trend, or extreme is what makes it *mean something*.

## Trend structure: higher highs and higher lows

Finally, the conceptual glue: an uptrend is a sequence of **higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL)**; a downtrend is **lower lows (LL) and lower highs (LH)**. This simple structure underlies everything:

- An uptrend is "alive" as long as it keeps making higher lows.
- A trend is "broken" when price makes a **lower low** (in an uptrend) — the point at which continuation strategies stand aside and reversion traders take over.

Reading HH/HL/LL/LH structure is the single most valuable skill in swing trading, and it is the entire subject of the next chapter.

## Summary

- Support/resistance are the levels where swings begin and end; they flip roles and are zones, not lines.
- Moving averages (20 EMA, 50/200) are dynamic support and the trend context.
- Oscillators (RSI, RSI-2) measure extremes — use them in ranges, not blindly in trends.
- Volume confirms; candlesticks trigger.
- Trend structure (HH/HL/LL/LH) is the underlying read that ties it all together.

# 3. Reading Trend Structure: Trending or Ranging?

## The decision that precedes every trade

Before any strategy, the swing trader must answer one question: **is this market trending or ranging?** The answer chooses *which* strategy to use — because the two families (continuation and reversion) are optimised for opposite conditions, and using the wrong one is the fastest way to lose money.

## The two regimes

- **Trending:** price moves persistently in one direction, making higher highs and higher lows (uptrend) or lower lows and lower highs (downtrend). **Continuation strategies win** (Chapters 4–6); reversion strategies get run over.
- **Ranging (consolidation):** price oscillates between a support floor and a resistance ceiling, making roughly equal highs and lows. **Reversion strategies win** (Chapters 7–8); continuation strategies whipsaw.

The catch: **markets spend a great deal of time ranging.** Trend-followers know this — that is why they expect many small losses and a few big winners. Swing traders, by contrast, have the luxury of *choosing the right tool for the regime* — but only if they can read the regime.

## How to read the regime

Three simple, visual checks:

### 1. The structure test

Look at the last several swing highs and swing lows:

- **Uptrend:** higher highs *and* higher lows.
- **Downtrend:** lower lows *and* lower highs.
- **Range:** horizontal highs and horizontal lows, price bouncing between two levels.

If the highs and lows are *stair-stepping* in one direction, it is a trend. If they are *sideways*, it is a range.

### 2. The moving-average test

- Price **above a rising 50-day MA** = uptrend.
- Price **below a falling 50-day MA** = downtrend.
- Price **chopping around a flat 50-day MA** = range.

A *sloping* average is the tell: a flat average means no trend.

### 3. The time test

- If price has been stuck between two visible levels for **many weeks**, it is a range.
- If price has been making progress in one direction for weeks, it is a trend.

The longer and tighter the range, the more energetic the eventual breakout — and the more dangerous it is to fade the edges blindly.

## Matching the strategy to the regime

| Regime | Use | Avoid |
|--------|-----|-------|
| Uptrend | Pullback continuation (Ch 4), breakout & retest (Ch 5), new-high momentum (Ch 6) | Range fade (Ch 8), blind RSI-2 shorts |
| Downtrend | Short-side continuation (mirror of Ch 4–6), or stand aside | Range fade longs, dip-buying |
| Range | RSI-2 mean reversion (Ch 7), range fade (Ch 8) | Breakout/continuation (whipsaw) |

Two honest notes:

1. **Downtrends are asymmetric in India.** Retail investors can *sell* what they own or use index shorts/puts, but the clean, liquid short-side is harder to access than the long side. Many Indian swing traders therefore simply **stand aside in downtrends** — which is itself a valid and profitable decision.
2. **Regimes change.** The single most dangerous moment is the *transition* — a range that is about to break out, or a trend that is about to reverse. At transitions, both strategy families look wrong at first. The discipline is to recognise the transition and *trade smaller or not at all* until the new regime confirms.

## A practical routine

Before every trade, in 30 seconds:

1. Pull up a daily chart.
2. Ask: are the highs and lows stair-stepping or sideways?
3. Glance at the 50-day MA: sloping or flat?
4. Label the regime: **uptrend, downtrend, or range.**
5. Choose the strategy family that matches — or stand aside if uncertain.

This routine, repeated thousands of times, is the difference between a trader who *adapts* and one who *hopes*.

## Summary

- Trending = HH/HL or LL/LH; ranging = sideways between two levels.
- Read it via structure, the slope of the 50-day MA, and the time test.
- Continuation strategies suit trends; reversion strategies suit ranges.
- Downtrends are asymmetric in India — standing aside is valid.
- The transition between regimes is the most dangerous moment; trade smaller or not at all.

# 4. Strategy 1 — The Pullback Continuation

## Buying the dip in an uptrend

The pullback continuation is the workhorse of trend-following swing trading. In an established uptrend, price routinely pulls back toward a rising moving average before resuming — and that pullback is a *pause*, not a reversal. This strategy buys the pause, in the direction of the trend.

## The idea

Trends advance in waves: up, small retreat, up again. The retreat (the **pullback**) is healthy — it shakes out weak holders and gives new buyers a lower entry. The pullback strategy formalises the oldest rule in trend trading: **in an uptrend, buy the dip; do not fight the trend.**

Because the strategy only trades *with* the trend, it enjoys a relatively high win rate — at the cost of occasionally missing a move (in a strong trend, price may not pull back to the entry level at all).

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks or index instruments. Daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — Confirm the uptrend.** Price must be **above a rising longer-term MA** (the 50-day is standard), and ideally making higher highs and higher lows.

**Step 2 — Wait for the pullback.** Price pulls back **toward the 20-day EMA** (the classic magnet) — a shallow, orderly retracement, *not* a collapse. The uptrend (50-day MA) must remain intact throughout.

**Step 3 — Enter on the resume.** Enter when the pullback shows a **reversal back up** — a bullish reversal candle off the 20-day EMA, or a close back above a short-term level. The candlestick is the trigger; the moving average is the context.

**Step 4 — Stop.** Place the stop **below the pullback low** (the lowest point of the retracement). If price falls below that low, the pullback was the start of a reversal, not a pause — exit.

**Step 5 — Target.** The natural target is the **prior swing high** (the high before the pullback), or a measured move beyond it. Take profit there; the strategy is about capturing one swing, not riding a trend forever.

## Historical context

"Buy the pullback in an uptrend" is one of the most durable and widely practised techniques in trading, and it is the practical expression of *trend continuation* — the documented tendency of intact trends to resume after pauses. Its logic is the same momentum persistence that underlies the momentum factor, applied to the multi-day timeframe. (It is the mirror image of fading a move: the same pullback that a continuation trader *buys* is what a reversion trader *fades* — the regime call from Chapter 3 decides who is right.)

## A worked example (a liquid NSE stock)

- A stock is in a clear uptrend, above a rising 50-day MA, having climbed from ₹400 to ₹500. The 20-day EMA is at ₹475 and rising.
- The stock **pulls back over a week to ₹478**, touching the rising 20-day EMA. The 50-day MA is still sloping up — uptrend intact.
- A **bullish reversal candle** prints at the 20-day EMA (a long lower shadow — buyers rejected the dip). **Entry** at ₹485.
- **Stop** at ₹468 (just below the pullback low of ₹472). Risk ≈ ₹17.
- The trend resumes and the stock rallies toward the prior high. **Target** at ₹500 (the prior swing high) — a modest reward-to-risk of roughly 0.9:1 by that measure alone (₹15 reward against ₹17 risk).

Result: a defined-risk trade in the direction of the trend, entered at a low-risk point near support. The reward-to-risk here is unremarkable if the trade stops exactly at the prior high — pullback trades are often taken specifically because a strong trend runs *past* the prior swing high before the next stall, which is what improves the realised ratio in practice. A trader who requires a better *stated* R:R before entry should look for a pullback with more room to the prior high, or trail the stop past the first target instead of taking it mechanically.

## Risks and limitations

- **Trend failure.** The defining risk: the "pullback" is actually the start of a reversal. The stop below the pullback low converts this into a small, defined loss — but in a genuine reversal, the loss is taken, not avoided.
- **Missing entries.** In strong trends, price may not reach the 20-day EMA, and the trader waits (or misses the move). This is the cost of demanding a better price.
- **Judgement required.** "Uptrend intact" and "reversal candle" are partly discretionary; the strategy needs written rules to stay consistent.
- **False reversals.** A reversal candle can fail; the stop is the final arbiter, and some failed entries are inevitable.

## Summary

- Buy pullbacks to the rising 20-day EMA within an uptrend (above the rising 50-day MA).
- Enter on a reversal candle; stop below the pullback low; target the prior swing high.
- High win rate, low-risk entry — but requires an existing trend and discipline.
- The mirror of fading; the trend-vs-range call decides which side you are on.

# 5. Strategy 2 — The Breakout and Retest

## The second, safer chance to enter a breakout

A breakout — price moving decisively through a resistance level — is a classic momentum signal, but buying the breakout itself is risky: breakouts often fail, and entering *into* the move means buying at the worst possible price. The **breakout and retest** strategy waits for a second, safer chance: after the breakout, price often **pulls back to retest the broken level** (now turned support) before continuing. That retest is the entry.

## The idea

The key concept is **role reversal**: when a resistance level is broken, it flips to become *support*. The logic is that the buyers who were trapped *above* the old resistance, and the sellers who sold *at* it, both now have reason to act at that level — creating a natural floor. A clean retest of the flipped level that *holds* is powerful confirmation that the breakout is genuine.

The strategy's appeal is a better entry: you buy **at the broken level** (near support), not at the breakout high — so the stop is tight and the reward-to-risk is favourable. The trade-off is that not every breakout *retests*; some just run away, and the trade is missed.

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks or index instruments, daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — Identify a resistance level.** A clearly defined horizontal level that has been tested at least twice (the more touches, the more meaningful).

**Step 2 — Wait for the breakout.** Price **closes above the resistance** on rising volume (volume confirms genuine participation, not a head-fake).

**Step 3 — Wait for the retest.** Price pulls back **toward the broken level** (the new support). This retest often comes over a few days.

**Step 4 — Enter on the hold.** Enter when the retest **holds** — a bullish reversal candle at the flipped level, or a close back above it. The level that was once resistance now supports.

**Step 5 — Stop.** Place the stop **below the retest low** (below the broken level). If price falls back *through* the level, the breakout has failed — exit.

**Step 6 — Target.** A **measured move**: project the height of the prior range (resistance − support) *upward* from the breakout level. Alternatively, the next resistance above.

## Historical context

The "breakout, then retest, then resume" sequence is one of the most widely documented patterns in technical trading, and it is fundamentally the same momentum-persistence idea as the other continuation strategies: a genuine breakout (new strength) is more likely to continue than to fail, and the retest is simply a *better entry* into that continuation. The technique's roots trace to classic price-action writing (the "springboard" / "retest of the breakout level" is a staple of the Wyckoff-style and chart-pattern literature), and it remains a standard swing entry today.

## A worked example (an NSE stock)

- A stock has been ranging between **₹200 (support) and ₹250 (resistance)** for months; the ₹250 level has been tested three times.
- On strong volume, the stock **closes at ₹258** — a breakout through ₹250.
- Over the next four days, price **pulls back to ₹251**, retesting the broken ₹250 level.
- At ₹251, a **bullish reversal candle** prints (buyers step in at the old resistance). **Entry** at ₹255.
- **Stop** at ₹244 (below the retest low and the ₹250 level).
- **Target:** the range height is ₹50 (250 − 200), so the measured target is 250 + 50 = **₹300**.

Result: the trader entered *near support* at ₹255 (not at the ₹258 breakout high), with a tight stop, targeting a measured move to ₹300.

## Risks and limitations

- **Failed retest.** The retest sometimes *does not hold* — price falls back through the level, and the trade stops out. This is the strategy's cost: some "breakouts" are false, and the retest is what reveals it.
- **No retest happens.** In strong breakouts, price may never pull back, and the trade is missed. The strategy sacrifices some winners for better entries on the ones it takes.
- **Loose levels.** A "level" is a zone; entering too precisely can lead to getting stopped by normal noise. Use a zone and a buffer.
- **Market dependence.** Breakouts (and their retests) succeed far more often in an uptrend than a downtrend; the broad-trend context still matters.

## Summary

- Wait for a breakout, then buy the retest of the broken level (now support).
- Enter on a reversal candle at the flipped level; stop below the retest low; target a measured move.
- A safer entry than buying the breakout itself, at the cost of missing breakouts that never retest.
- Role reversal — broken resistance becomes support — is the core concept.

# 6. Strategy 3 — The New-High Momentum Trade

## Buying strength itself

The new-high momentum trade is the most aggressive of the continuation strategies: it buys a stock making a **new high** (a 52-week high, or a multi-month high) and rides the subsequent momentum, rather than waiting for a pullback. It is the swing-trading application of the documented 52-week-high momentum effect.

## The idea

Conventional wisdom says a stock at a new high is "too high." The evidence says the opposite: stocks at or near their 52-week highs have historically been *strong*, not overpriced — because the forces that pushed them to new highs (earnings, news, institutional accumulation) tend to persist. The new-high trade buys that persistence directly.

The psychological foundation is **anchoring and under-reaction**: investors are slow to update their view of a stock that has broken above everything they remember, so the move is not fully priced and has room to continue.

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks (or indices) with a genuine catalyst — the strategy works best on names showing real strength, not sleepy ones. Daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — Identify the high.** A stock is at or near its **52-week high** (or a clearly significant multi-month high).

**Step 2 — Wait for the breakout.** Enter when the stock **closes at a new high** — ideally on **rising volume** (participation confirms the move). Some traders add a small confirmation margin (close a little *above* the prior high) to avoid intraday head-fakes.

**Step 3 — Stop.** Place the stop a defined distance below entry — e.g., a close below the **prior high** (the breakout level), or a fixed 6–10% stop, or below a short-term MA (the 10-day low). The stop's job is to exit fast if the breakout fails.

**Step 4 — Target.** New-high momentum has no obvious resistance above (that is the point), so the exit is usually **trailing** — e.g., a close below the 10-day or 20-day low, or a fixed time stop (exit after N days if the move has not continued). Letting the winner run is the source of the edge.

**Position sizing:** because this is a breakout with no support beneath, keep the risk per trade small (the standard 1–2%).

## Historical context

The empirical basis is the same George & Hwang (2004) finding used in the momentum-investing book: nearness to the 52-week high is one of the most robust momentum signals in the literature. Applied at the swing timeframe, it becomes a "buy strength and trail" trade. The honest caveat is the same: *historically, stocks breaking to new highs have tended to continue outperforming — but individual breakouts fail often, so the stop and diversification are what make it survivable.*

## A worked example (an NSE stock)

- A large-cap stock, after a long base, **closes at ₹1,120 — a new 52-week high** — on volume 1.5× its average. **Entry** at ₹1,120.
- **Stop** placed at ₹1,045 (a close below the prior-high/breakout zone, ~7% risk).
- The stock trends for five weeks, reaching ₹1,380, making higher highs throughout.
- A pullback then closes the stock **below its 20-day low at ₹1,290** — the trailing exit fires. **Exit** at ₹1,290.

Result: entry ₹1,120 → exit ₹1,290 = **+15%**, captured by buying strength and trailing the stop — with a defined risk of ~7% if the breakout had failed.

## Risks and limitations

- **High failure rate.** Many new-high breakouts reverse immediately. The win rate is low; the edge comes from the occasional big winner *outweighing* the many small losses. This demands strict stops and small position sizing.
- **Gap risk.** A stock gapping up to a new high on news can reverse the same day; buying *into* the gap is riskier than a clean close above the level.
- **Trailing-exit discipline.** The strategy only works if winners are *not* cut early; a trailing exit (not a fixed profit) is essential, and it is psychologically hard (Chapter 10).
- **Market dependence.** New highs succeed far more often in a rising market; a broad-market filter improves the odds materially.

## Summary

- Buy closes at new 52-week (or significant) highs on rising volume.
- Stop below the breakout level or a fixed %; exit on a trailing stop, not a target.
- Grounded in the George & Hwang (2004) 52-week-high momentum effect.
- Low win rate, large winners — strict stops and small size are non-negotiable.

# 7. Strategy 4 — RSI-2 Mean Reversion

## Fading the short-term extreme

The first three strategies were *continuation* — they buy strength and follow the trend. This chapter switches to the second engine of swing trading: **mean reversion**. The RSI-2 strategy buys when a stock is *short-term oversold* and expects a snap-back. It is the most famous documented short-term mean-reversion system.

## The idea

Markets overshoot. In the short run, a sharp sell-off often pushes a stock *too far, too fast* — and prices tend to **snap back** toward the average, even if the longer-term trend is down. The RSI-2 strategy captures these bounces.

The key instrument is the **2-period RSI (RSI-2)** — an *extremely* short-term oscillator that flips to deeply oversold (near 0) very quickly on a sharp drop. The strategy, documented by Larry Connors and Cesar Alvarez in their book *Short Term Trading Strategies That Work*, buys those deeply oversold moments and exits a few days later on the bounce.

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks (or liquid index instruments). Daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — The long-term trend filter (critical).** Only trade in the direction of the longer trend. The classic version: take **longs only when the stock is above its 200-day MA** (an uptrend). In a downtrend, the same setup is used for shorts (or the trade is simply skipped). This filter is *why the strategy works* — buying oversold bounces in an uptrend is high-probability; buying them in a downtrend is catching falling knives.

**Step 2 — The trigger.** Enter a **long when RSI-2 closes below 5** (some use below 10), signalling a deeply oversold short-term extreme.

**Step 3 — Exit.** Exit when price **closes above the 5-day MA** (the classic rule), or after a fixed number of days (e.g., 2–5), or at a fixed small profit target. The trade is a *short* bounce, held only days.

**Step 4 — Stop.** A stop below the entry day's low (or a fixed small percentage), for the times the bounce does not come.

**Position sizing:** standard small risk per trade; the strategy trades frequently, so costs matter.

## Historical context

Connors and Alvarez published extensive historical results for RSI-2 mean reversion across US equities, showing that a simple "buy RSI-2 < 5, exit above the 5-day MA" rule — *crucially, filtered by the 200-day trend* — produced a high win rate over many years. The mechanism is well understood: short-term liquidity-driven overshoots revert, and the trend filter keeps the trader from fighting a genuine downtrend. The pattern generalises: *short-term extremes tend to revert, especially with the longer-term trend.*

Framed honestly: *historically, buying deeply short-term-oversold conditions in assets above their long-term trend has tended to produce frequent small wins — with occasional larger losses when the "bounce" turns out to be a breakdown, which the stop is there to cap.*

## A worked example (a liquid NSE stock)

- A large-cap stock is in an uptrend, trading **above its 200-day MA** at ₹800.
- Over three days it **drops sharply to ₹740** on broad market weakness. Its **RSI-2 closes at 3** — deeply oversold. **Entry** at ₹742.
- The 200-day MA is still well below — the longer trend is intact; this is a dip, not a reversal.
- Over the next three days the stock **bounces and closes above its 5-day MA at ₹775**. **Exit** at ₹775.

Result: entry ₹742 → exit ₹775 = **+4.4% in a few days**, a short, high-probability bounce captured with a defined stop and a mechanical exit.

## Risks and limitations

- **Catching falling knives.** Without the 200-day filter, this strategy buys every crash and loses badly. **The trend filter is not optional — it is the strategy.**
- **Frequent small losses.** Bounces sometimes fail; the stop caps each loss, but the strategy can string together several small losers. It relies on a high win rate *with the filter* to stay net positive.
- **Transaction costs.** RSI-2 trades frequently (often holding only days), so brokerage and STT on each round trip matter more than for slower strategies. Run it on liquid names.
- **False confidence in high win rates.** A high win rate can hide a few larger losses; risk per trade must remain small and disciplined.

## Summary

- Buy RSI-2 closes below 5 (or 10), exit above the 5-day MA — held only days.
- The 200-day trend filter is what makes it work; without it, you are catching knives.
- Documented by Connors & Alvarez; short-term extremes revert.
- High win rate, frequent small trades — costs and stop discipline matter.

# 8. Strategy 5 — The Range Fade

## Selling the edges of the box

The range fade is the purest mean-reversion strategy: in a market that is clearly **range-bound**, it buys at support and sells at resistance — *fading* (trading against) each move toward the edges of the range. It is the natural complement to the continuation strategies: it makes money precisely in the conditions where they whipsaw.

## The idea

A ranging market oscillates between two well-defined levels. Near the **top** of the range, buyers have exhausted themselves and price is likely to fall back; near the **bottom**, sellers have exhausted themselves and price is likely to bounce. The range fade simply **buys the bottom edge and sells the top edge**, repeatedly, as long as the range holds.

The logic is mean reversion at its cleanest: *extremes within a bounded structure tend to revert to the middle.* The entire risk is that the range **breaks** — and one large loss from a breakout must not be allowed to erase many small wins.

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks or index instruments, daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — Identify a clear range.** Price must be **visibly range-bound**: a horizontal support floor and resistance ceiling, each tested at least twice, price bouncing between them for several weeks. A flat 50-day MA confirms "no trend."

**Step 2 — Enter at the edge.**
- **Long:** near the **support** floor, ideally with a reversal candle (buyers stepping in).
- **Short / exit long:** near the **resistance** ceiling, ideally with a reversal candle (sellers stepping in).

**Step 3 — Stop.** Just **outside the range**: for a long, below support; for a short, above resistance. The stop's job is to exit the moment the range *breaks* — because a broken range becomes a trend, and the fade must not fight it.

**Step 4 — Target.** The **opposite edge** of the range (or its middle). Take profit there; the strategy is harvesting the oscillation, not predicting a breakout.

**Position sizing:** small per trade, because the win rate is decent but the *occasional breakout loss* is the risk that dominates.

## Historical context

Range fading is the oldest mean-reversion trade there is — the practical expression of the observation that, in the absence of a trend, prices oscillate around a mean and revert at the edges. It is the mirror image of breakout trading: the same level that a breakout trader *buys through* is the level a range fader *sells into*. Neither is right or wrong in the abstract; the market's **regime** (Chapter 3) decides which one pays. The strategy is as old as support-and-resistance itself, and it remains a staple precisely because markets spend so much time ranging.

Framed honestly: *historically, in clearly range-bound conditions, fading the edges of the range has tended to produce frequent small wins — punctuated by occasional large losses when the range breaks, which the stop is there to cap.*

## A worked example (BankNIFTY or a liquid stock)

- A stock has been ranging between **₹500 (support)** and **₹560 (resistance)** for two months, each level tested three times. The 50-day MA is flat.
- Price falls to **₹505** and prints a **bullish reversal candle** (a hammer — buyers rejected the dip). **Entry** at ₹510 (long).
- **Stop** at ₹492 (just below the ₹500 support floor).
- Price rallies toward the top of the range. At **₹555**, near resistance, a **bearish reversal candle** prints. **Exit** at ₹552.

Result: entry ₹510 → exit ₹552 = **+8.2%**, harvesting one full oscillation of the range with a stop that capped the risk if support had broken.

## Risks and limitations

- **The breakout loss.** The defining risk: the range breaks, the fade is wrong, and the stop exits at a loss. The *many small wins* must outweigh the *occasional breakout loss* — which requires strict stops and small size, not cleverness.
- **False range reads.** A market that *looks* like a range may actually be a trend consolidating before continuation. Fading it then means fighting the trend. The flat-50-day-MA check and clear double-tested levels are the guards.
- **Erosion at the edges.** Repeated fades at the same edge become increasingly risky — the more a level is tested, the more likely it is to break. Some traders reduce size on later tests.
- **Costs and patience.** The strategy trades repeatedly for small gains; costs and the discipline to take the small win matter.

## Summary

- In a clear range, buy support and sell resistance, fading the edges.
- Stop just outside the range; target the opposite edge.
- The mirror of breakout trading; the regime call decides which pays.
- Many small wins, occasional breakout loss — strict stops and small size are essential.

# 9. Trade Plans and Risk Management

## Turning a setup into a repeatable process

A strategy is a set of ideas; a **trade plan** turns it into a repeatable, checkable process. This chapter covers the two things that separate profitable swing traders from everyone else: the **written plan**, and the **risk arithmetic** that keeps them alive.

## The written trade plan

Every trade, before it exists, should be written down with these six fields. The act of writing is what forces the thinking.

1. **Setup** — which strategy, and the specific condition (pullback to the 20 EMA, breakout of ₹250, RSI-2 below 5, etc.).
2. **Entry** — the exact trigger (close above the level, reversal candle at the MA).
3. **Stop** — the price that proves the setup wrong (below the pullback low, below the retest low, below the range floor).
4. **Target** — where the swing is likely to exhaust (prior high, measured move, opposite range edge, or the trailing rule).
5. **Size** — the position size, computed from the stop distance (below).
6. **Invalidation** — the conditions under which the plan is abandoned *before* the stop (e.g., the trade becomes invalid if a major event intervenes).

A plan this explicit does three jobs: it removes emotion from the decision, it makes the risk *known in advance*, and it makes the trade *reviewable* afterward — which is how a trader improves.

## The risk arithmetic: reward-to-risk

The central number in swing trading is the **reward-to-risk ratio (R:R)**:

R:R = (Target − Entry) ÷ (Entry − Stop)

It measures how much you stand to gain for every unit you risk. The discipline of swing trading is built on it:

- A trade risking ₹10 to make ₹30 is a **3:1** trade.
- A **3:1** trade needs to win only **~25%** of the time to break even.
- A **1:1** trade needs to win **~50%** of the time.

This is why swing trading works with a *modest* win rate: **you do not need to be right most of the time — you need to win enough on the winners to pay for the losers.** The pullback, retest, and range-fade strategies all aim for favourable R:R precisely so that the maths works in your favour.

The corollary is unforgiving: **if you cut winners early and let losers run, you invert the R:R — and no strategy can save you.** Protecting the ratio (cut losers fast, hold to targets) is the whole game.

## Position sizing: risk 1–2% per trade

The formula from the other books applies unchanged:

Position size = (Account × Risk %) ÷ (Entry − Stop)

Risking **1–2%** of the account per trade means **no single loss — and no losing streak — can meaningfully damage the account.** For swing trading, where a string of stopped-out trades is normal, this is not a suggestion; it is the load-bearing rule. A trader who risks 1% can survive ten consecutive losses (down ~10%) and keep trading; a trader who risks 10% is ruined by the same streak.

## The risks specific to swing trading

### Gap risk (the overnight risk)

Swing traders hold overnight, so positions are exposed to **gaps** — price opening far from the previous close on news or a global shock. A gap can jump *past* the stop, producing a larger-than-planned loss. Defences:

- **Size for the worst plausible gap**, not just the stop distance.
- **Avoid oversized positions through binary events** (earnings, budgets, policy decisions) on the underlying.
- Accept that the stop is a *limit*, not a *guarantee*.

### The losing streak

Every strategy has streaks of consecutive losses — continuation strategies whipsaw in ranges; reversion strategies get run over at breakouts. The defence is **survival**: a 1% risk per trade turns a 10-loss streak into a survivable 10% drawdown, after which the winners can resume.

### The transition risk

The most dangerous moment is a **regime change** — a range breaking into a trend, or a trend reversing. Both families of strategy look wrong at the transition. The defence is the regime discipline of Chapter 3: when the regime is ambiguous, **trade smaller or not at all.**

## Portfolio-level discipline

Beyond the individual trade:

- **Limit total open risk.** A handful of open positions each risking 1% is fine; ten correlated positions all long the same market is one oversized bet.
- **Know your net exposure.** If every position is long and the market is below its 200-day MA, the whole book is a single bet against the trend — reduce it.
- **Review, don't rationalise.** After each closed trade, check the plan against what you actually did. The gap between them is where improvement lives.

## Summary

- Write a six-field plan (setup, entry, stop, target, size, invalidation) before every trade.
- Reward-to-risk is the core arithmetic: a 3:1 trade breaks even at 25% wins.
- Risk 1–2% per trade; size from the stop distance.
- Gap risk, losing streaks, and regime transitions are the specific dangers — survive them with small size and regime discipline.
- Review every trade against its plan.

# 10. Psychology and Common Mistakes

## The discipline between the entries and the exits

The strategies in this book are public knowledge, and the risk arithmetic is simple. What decides whether a swing trader profits is what happens **between** the entry and the exit — the hours and days of holding, during which every position tests the trader's nerve. This chapter names the traps and the defences.

## Why holding is the hard part

Swing trading is psychologically difficult for a specific reason: **it demands patience and inaction at the exact moments the emotions are loudest.**

- A position in **profit** begs to be closed ("take it before it disappears") — but the plan says hold to the target.
- A position in **drawdown** (but not at the stop) begs to be closed ("cut it now") — but the plan says the stop, not the noise, is the exit.
- A stopped-out position begs to be **re-entered** immediately ("get it back") — but the plan says the next trade comes from the next setup.

In every case, the correct action is *nothing* — and the temptation is to *do something*. The trader who can hold through the noise, honour the stop, and wait for the next setup has the edge.

## The five classic mistakes

### 1. Cutting winners early

The whole edge of swing trading is the reward-to-risk ratio — the winners must be allowed to reach their targets. Closing a winner early ("locking in a small profit") takes exactly the trades that pay for the losers. **Defence:** the target (or the trailing rule) ends the trade, not the profit.

### 2. Moving the stop

A position near its stop gets "just a little more room" — and a small, planned loss becomes a large, unplanned one. **Defence:** the stop is a commitment made in a calm state; it is honoured in the emotional state. The stop *is* the plan.

### 3. Revenge trading after a stop-out

A stopped-out trade is almost always followed by the urge to re-enter immediately, bigger, "to win it back." This converts one defined loss into an undefined, oversized one — the single most destructive habit in trading. **Defence:** after a stop, the correct next trade is **no trade**, until a fresh setup presents itself on its own merits.

### 4. Overtrading the wrong regime

When the market is ranging, continuation strategies whipsaw; when it is trending, reversion strategies get run over. The trader who ignores the regime and trades *more* to "fix" a losing streak is doing the worst thing at the worst time. **Defence:** the regime discipline of Chapter 3 — trade the matching strategy, and trade less (or not at all) when the regime is ambiguous.

### 5. Confusing one trade's outcome with skill

A single winning trade feels like talent; a single losing trade feels like failure. Both are mostly noise. **Defence:** judge yourself over 30–50 trades, on the *process* (did I follow the plan?) rather than the outcome. A losing trade that followed the plan is a process win.

## The practices that work

1. **Write the plan, then follow it.** The written plan (Chapter 9) is a contract with your future, more emotional self.
2. **Set exits at entry.** The stop and target are decided *before* the position exists, when judgment is clean — never adjusted mid-trade.
3. **Trade a defined routine.** Same chart review, same checklist, same time of day. Routine reduces the scope for impulsive decisions.
4. **Accept the losing streak as the cost.** Streaks of stopped-out trades are normal and expected. A trader who accepts them can hold through them; one who fights them will abandon the system at the bottom.
5. **Review, don't rationalise.** After each trade, compare what you did against the plan. The gap is where you improve — and rationalising it away is where you don't.

## The honest truth

Every failure mode in this chapter is a way of *abandoning your own plan at the moment it matters most*. Swing trading's edge is real but modest, and it is expressed only across many disciplined trades. The trader who can simply **do what they said they would do** — take the setup, honour the stop, hold to the target, wait for the next one — has an edge that no amount of strategy sophistication can replace. Everything else is detail.

## Summary

- The hard part is the *holding* — doing nothing when the emotions are loudest.
- The traps: cutting winners, moving stops, revenge trading, overtrading the wrong regime, confusing luck with skill.
- The defences: a written plan, exits set at entry, a defined routine, accepting losing streaks, reviewing honestly.
- Discipline — not a better strategy — is the actual edge.

## The two families

- **Continuation (1–3):** trade *with* the trend — buy strength, buy pauses, buy new highs. Win rate often lower; winners are larger.

## The universal rules

- **Read the regime first** (trending or ranging) — it chooses the family.
- **Risk 1–2%** per trade; size from the stop distance.

- **Honour the stop; hold to the target** — never invert the ratio.
