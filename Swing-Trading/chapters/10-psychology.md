# 10. Psychology and Common Mistakes

## The discipline between the entries and the exits

The strategies in this book are public knowledge, and the risk arithmetic is simple. What decides whether a swing trader profits is what happens **between** the entry and the exit — the hours and days of holding, during which every position tests the trader's nerve. This chapter names the traps and the defences.

## Why holding is the hard part

Swing trading is psychologically difficult for a specific reason: **it demands patience and inaction at the exact moments the emotions are loudest.**

- A position in **profit** begs to be closed ("take it before it disappears") — but the plan says hold to the target.
- A position in **drawdown** (but not at the stop) begs to be closed ("cut it now") — but the plan says the stop, not the noise, is the exit.
- A stopped-out position begs to be **re-entered** immediately ("get it back") — but the plan says the next trade comes from the next setup.

In every case, the correct action is *nothing* — and the temptation is to *do something*. The trader who can hold through the noise, honour the stop, and wait for the next setup has the edge.

## The five classic mistakes

### 1. Cutting winners early

The whole edge of swing trading is the reward-to-risk ratio — the winners must be allowed to reach their targets. Closing a winner early ("locking in a small profit") takes exactly the trades that pay for the losers. **Defence:** the target (or the trailing rule) ends the trade, not the profit.

### 2. Moving the stop

A position near its stop gets "just a little more room" — and a small, planned loss becomes a large, unplanned one. **Defence:** the stop is a commitment made in a calm state; it is honoured in the emotional state. The stop *is* the plan.

### 3. Revenge trading after a stop-out

A stopped-out trade is almost always followed by the urge to re-enter immediately, bigger, "to win it back." This converts one defined loss into an undefined, oversized one — the single most destructive habit in trading. **Defence:** after a stop, the correct next trade is **no trade**, until a fresh setup presents itself on its own merits.

### 4. Overtrading the wrong regime

When the market is ranging, continuation strategies whipsaw; when it is trending, reversion strategies get run over. The trader who ignores the regime and trades *more* to "fix" a losing streak is doing the worst thing at the worst time. **Defence:** the regime discipline of Chapter 3 — trade the matching strategy, and trade less (or not at all) when the regime is ambiguous.

### 5. Confusing one trade's outcome with skill

A single winning trade feels like talent; a single losing trade feels like failure. Both are mostly noise. **Defence:** judge yourself over 30–50 trades, on the *process* (did I follow the plan?) rather than the outcome. A losing trade that followed the plan is a process win.

## The practices that work

1. **Write the plan, then follow it.** The written plan (Chapter 9) is a contract with your future, more emotional self.
2. **Set exits at entry.** The stop and target are decided *before* the position exists, when judgment is clean — never adjusted mid-trade.
3. **Trade a defined routine.** Same chart review, same checklist, same time of day. Routine reduces the scope for impulsive decisions.
4. **Accept the losing streak as the cost.** Streaks of stopped-out trades are normal and expected. A trader who accepts them can hold through them; one who fights them will abandon the system at the bottom.
5. **Review, don't rationalise.** After each trade, compare what you did against the plan. The gap is where you improve — and rationalising it away is where you don't.

## The honest truth

Every failure mode in this chapter is a way of *abandoning your own plan at the moment it matters most*. Swing trading's edge is real but modest, and it is expressed only across many disciplined trades. The trader who can simply **do what they said they would do** — take the setup, honour the stop, hold to the target, wait for the next one — has an edge that no amount of strategy sophistication can replace. Everything else is detail.

## Summary

- The hard part is the *holding* — doing nothing when the emotions are loudest.
- The traps: cutting winners, moving stops, revenge trading, overtrading the wrong regime, confusing luck with skill.
- The defences: a written plan, exits set at entry, a defined routine, accepting losing streaks, reviewing honestly.
- Discipline — not a better strategy — is the actual edge.
