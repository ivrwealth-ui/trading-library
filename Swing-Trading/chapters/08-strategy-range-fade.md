# 8. Strategy 5 — The Range Fade

## Selling the edges of the box

The range fade is the purest mean-reversion strategy: in a market that is clearly **range-bound**, it buys at support and sells at resistance — *fading* (trading against) each move toward the edges of the range. It is the natural complement to the continuation strategies: it makes money precisely in the conditions where they whipsaw.

## The idea

A ranging market oscillates between two well-defined levels. Near the **top** of the range, buyers have exhausted themselves and price is likely to fall back; near the **bottom**, sellers have exhausted themselves and price is likely to bounce. The range fade simply **buys the bottom edge and sells the top edge**, repeatedly, as long as the range holds.

The logic is mean reversion at its cleanest: *extremes within a bounded structure tend to revert to the middle.* The entire risk is that the range **breaks** — and one large loss from a breakout must not be allowed to erase many small wins.

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks or index instruments, daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — Identify a clear range.** Price must be **visibly range-bound**: a horizontal support floor and resistance ceiling, each tested at least twice, price bouncing between them for several weeks. A flat 50-day MA confirms "no trend."

**Step 2 — Enter at the edge.**
- **Long:** near the **support** floor, ideally with a reversal candle (buyers stepping in).
- **Short / exit long:** near the **resistance** ceiling, ideally with a reversal candle (sellers stepping in).

**Step 3 — Stop.** Just **outside the range**: for a long, below support; for a short, above resistance. The stop's job is to exit the moment the range *breaks* — because a broken range becomes a trend, and the fade must not fight it.

**Step 4 — Target.** The **opposite edge** of the range (or its middle). Take profit there; the strategy is harvesting the oscillation, not predicting a breakout.

**Position sizing:** small per trade, because the win rate is decent but the *occasional breakout loss* is the risk that dominates.

## Historical context

Range fading is the oldest mean-reversion trade there is — the practical expression of the observation that, in the absence of a trend, prices oscillate around a mean and revert at the edges. It is the mirror image of breakout trading: the same level that a breakout trader *buys through* is the level a range fader *sells into*. Neither is right or wrong in the abstract; the market's **regime** (Chapter 3) decides which one pays. The strategy is as old as support-and-resistance itself, and it remains a staple precisely because markets spend so much time ranging.

Framed honestly: *historically, in clearly range-bound conditions, fading the edges of the range has tended to produce frequent small wins — punctuated by occasional large losses when the range breaks, which the stop is there to cap.*

## A worked example (BankNIFTY or a liquid stock)

- A stock has been ranging between **₹500 (support)** and **₹560 (resistance)** for two months, each level tested three times. The 50-day MA is flat.
- Price falls to **₹505** and prints a **bullish reversal candle** (a hammer — buyers rejected the dip). **Entry** at ₹510 (long).
- **Stop** at ₹492 (just below the ₹500 support floor).
- Price rallies toward the top of the range. At **₹555**, near resistance, a **bearish reversal candle** prints. **Exit** at ₹552.

Result: entry ₹510 → exit ₹552 = **+8.2%**, harvesting one full oscillation of the range with a stop that capped the risk if support had broken.

## Risks and limitations

- **The breakout loss.** The defining risk: the range breaks, the fade is wrong, and the stop exits at a loss. The *many small wins* must outweigh the *occasional breakout loss* — which requires strict stops and small size, not cleverness.
- **False range reads.** A market that *looks* like a range may actually be a trend consolidating before continuation. Fading it then means fighting the trend. The flat-50-day-MA check and clear double-tested levels are the guards.
- **Erosion at the edges.** Repeated fades at the same edge become increasingly risky — the more a level is tested, the more likely it is to break. Some traders reduce size on later tests.
- **Costs and patience.** The strategy trades repeatedly for small gains; costs and the discipline to take the small win matter.

## Summary

- In a clear range, buy support and sell resistance, fading the edges.
- Stop just outside the range; target the opposite edge.
- The mirror of breakout trading; the regime call decides which pays.
- Many small wins, occasional breakout loss — strict stops and small size are essential.
