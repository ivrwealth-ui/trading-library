# 3. Reading Trend Structure: Trending or Ranging?

## The decision that precedes every trade

Before any strategy, the swing trader must answer one question: **is this market trending or ranging?** The answer chooses *which* strategy to use — because the two families (continuation and reversion) are optimised for opposite conditions, and using the wrong one is the fastest way to lose money.

## The two regimes

- **Trending:** price moves persistently in one direction, making higher highs and higher lows (uptrend) or lower lows and lower highs (downtrend). **Continuation strategies win** (Chapters 4–6); reversion strategies get run over.
- **Ranging (consolidation):** price oscillates between a support floor and a resistance ceiling, making roughly equal highs and lows. **Reversion strategies win** (Chapters 7–8); continuation strategies whipsaw.

The catch: **markets spend a great deal of time ranging.** Trend-followers know this — that is why they expect many small losses and a few big winners. Swing traders, by contrast, have the luxury of *choosing the right tool for the regime* — but only if they can read the regime.

## How to read the regime

Three simple, visual checks:

### 1. The structure test

Look at the last several swing highs and swing lows:

- **Uptrend:** higher highs *and* higher lows.
- **Downtrend:** lower lows *and* lower highs.
- **Range:** horizontal highs and horizontal lows, price bouncing between two levels.

If the highs and lows are *stair-stepping* in one direction, it is a trend. If they are *sideways*, it is a range.

### 2. The moving-average test

- Price **above a rising 50-day MA** = uptrend.
- Price **below a falling 50-day MA** = downtrend.
- Price **chopping around a flat 50-day MA** = range.

A *sloping* average is the tell: a flat average means no trend.

### 3. The time test

- If price has been stuck between two visible levels for **many weeks**, it is a range.
- If price has been making progress in one direction for weeks, it is a trend.

The longer and tighter the range, the more energetic the eventual breakout — and the more dangerous it is to fade the edges blindly.

## Matching the strategy to the regime

| Regime | Use | Avoid |
|--------|-----|-------|
| Uptrend | Pullback continuation (Ch 4), breakout & retest (Ch 5), new-high momentum (Ch 6) | Range fade (Ch 8), blind RSI-2 shorts |
| Downtrend | Short-side continuation (mirror of Ch 4–6), or stand aside | Range fade longs, dip-buying |
| Range | RSI-2 mean reversion (Ch 7), range fade (Ch 8) | Breakout/continuation (whipsaw) |

Two honest notes:

1. **Downtrends are asymmetric in India.** Retail investors can *sell* what they own or use index shorts/puts, but the clean, liquid short-side is harder to access than the long side. Many Indian swing traders therefore simply **stand aside in downtrends** — which is itself a valid and profitable decision.
2. **Regimes change.** The single most dangerous moment is the *transition* — a range that is about to break out, or a trend that is about to reverse. At transitions, both strategy families look wrong at first. The discipline is to recognise the transition and *trade smaller or not at all* until the new regime confirms.

## A practical routine

Before every trade, in 30 seconds:

1. Pull up a daily chart.
2. Ask: are the highs and lows stair-stepping or sideways?
3. Glance at the 50-day MA: sloping or flat?
4. Label the regime: **uptrend, downtrend, or range.**
5. Choose the strategy family that matches — or stand aside if uncertain.

This routine, repeated thousands of times, is the difference between a trader who *adapts* and one who *hopes*.

## Summary

- Trending = HH/HL or LL/LH; ranging = sideways between two levels.
- Read it via structure, the slope of the 50-day MA, and the time test.
- Continuation strategies suit trends; reversion strategies suit ranges.
- Downtrends are asymmetric in India — standing aside is valid.
- The transition between regimes is the most dangerous moment; trade smaller or not at all.
