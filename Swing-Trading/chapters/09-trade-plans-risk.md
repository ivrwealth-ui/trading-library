# 9. Trade Plans and Risk Management

## Turning a setup into a repeatable process

A strategy is a set of ideas; a **trade plan** turns it into a repeatable, checkable process. This chapter covers the two things that separate profitable swing traders from everyone else: the **written plan**, and the **risk arithmetic** that keeps them alive.

## The written trade plan

Every trade, before it exists, should be written down with these six fields. The act of writing is what forces the thinking.

1. **Setup** — which strategy, and the specific condition (pullback to the 20 EMA, breakout of ₹250, RSI-2 below 5, etc.).
2. **Entry** — the exact trigger (close above the level, reversal candle at the MA).
3. **Stop** — the price that proves the setup wrong (below the pullback low, below the retest low, below the range floor).
4. **Target** — where the swing is likely to exhaust (prior high, measured move, opposite range edge, or the trailing rule).
5. **Size** — the position size, computed from the stop distance (below).
6. **Invalidation** — the conditions under which the plan is abandoned *before* the stop (e.g., the trade becomes invalid if a major event intervenes).

A plan this explicit does three jobs: it removes emotion from the decision, it makes the risk *known in advance*, and it makes the trade *reviewable* afterward — which is how a trader improves.

## The risk arithmetic: reward-to-risk

The central number in swing trading is the **reward-to-risk ratio (R:R)**:

R:R = (Target − Entry) ÷ (Entry − Stop)

It measures how much you stand to gain for every unit you risk. The discipline of swing trading is built on it:

- A trade risking ₹10 to make ₹30 is a **3:1** trade.
- A **3:1** trade needs to win only **~25%** of the time to break even.
- A **1:1** trade needs to win **~50%** of the time.

This is why swing trading works with a *modest* win rate: **you do not need to be right most of the time — you need to win enough on the winners to pay for the losers.** The pullback, retest, and range-fade strategies all aim for favourable R:R precisely so that the maths works in your favour.

The corollary is unforgiving: **if you cut winners early and let losers run, you invert the R:R — and no strategy can save you.** Protecting the ratio (cut losers fast, hold to targets) is the whole game.

## Position sizing: risk 1–2% per trade

The formula from the other books applies unchanged:

Position size = (Account × Risk %) ÷ (Entry − Stop)

Risking **1–2%** of the account per trade means **no single loss — and no losing streak — can meaningfully damage the account.** For swing trading, where a string of stopped-out trades is normal, this is not a suggestion; it is the load-bearing rule. A trader who risks 1% can survive ten consecutive losses (down ~10%) and keep trading; a trader who risks 10% is ruined by the same streak.

## The risks specific to swing trading

### Gap risk (the overnight risk)

Swing traders hold overnight, so positions are exposed to **gaps** — price opening far from the previous close on news or a global shock. A gap can jump *past* the stop, producing a larger-than-planned loss. Defences:

- **Size for the worst plausible gap**, not just the stop distance.
- **Avoid oversized positions through binary events** (earnings, budgets, policy decisions) on the underlying.
- Accept that the stop is a *limit*, not a *guarantee*.

### The losing streak

Every strategy has streaks of consecutive losses — continuation strategies whipsaw in ranges; reversion strategies get run over at breakouts. The defence is **survival**: a 1% risk per trade turns a 10-loss streak into a survivable 10% drawdown, after which the winners can resume.

### The transition risk

The most dangerous moment is a **regime change** — a range breaking into a trend, or a trend reversing. Both families of strategy look wrong at the transition. The defence is the regime discipline of Chapter 3: when the regime is ambiguous, **trade smaller or not at all.**

## Portfolio-level discipline

Beyond the individual trade:

- **Limit total open risk.** A handful of open positions each risking 1% is fine; ten correlated positions all long the same market is one oversized bet.
- **Know your net exposure.** If every position is long and the market is below its 200-day MA, the whole book is a single bet against the trend — reduce it.
- **Review, don't rationalise.** After each closed trade, check the plan against what you actually did. The gap between them is where improvement lives.

## Summary

- Write a six-field plan (setup, entry, stop, target, size, invalidation) before every trade.
- Reward-to-risk is the core arithmetic: a 3:1 trade breaks even at 25% wins.
- Risk 1–2% per trade; size from the stop distance.
- Gap risk, losing streaks, and regime transitions are the specific dangers — survive them with small size and regime discipline.
- Review every trade against its plan.
