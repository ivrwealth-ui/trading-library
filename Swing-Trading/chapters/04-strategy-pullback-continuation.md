# 4. Strategy 1 — The Pullback Continuation

## Buying the dip in an uptrend

The pullback continuation is the workhorse of trend-following swing trading. In an established uptrend, price routinely pulls back toward a rising moving average before resuming — and that pullback is a *pause*, not a reversal. This strategy buys the pause, in the direction of the trend.

## The idea

Trends advance in waves: up, small retreat, up again. The retreat (the **pullback**) is healthy — it shakes out weak holders and gives new buyers a lower entry. The pullback strategy formalises the oldest rule in trend trading: **in an uptrend, buy the dip; do not fight the trend.**

Because the strategy only trades *with* the trend, it enjoys a relatively high win rate — at the cost of occasionally missing a move (in a strong trend, price may not pull back to the entry level at all).

## The rules

**Universe and timeframe:** liquid stocks or index instruments. Daily timeframe.

**Step 1 — Confirm the uptrend.** Price must be **above a rising longer-term MA** (the 50-day is standard), and ideally making higher highs and higher lows.

**Step 2 — Wait for the pullback.** Price pulls back **toward the 20-day EMA** (the classic magnet) — a shallow, orderly retracement, *not* a collapse. The uptrend (50-day MA) must remain intact throughout.

**Step 3 — Enter on the resume.** Enter when the pullback shows a **reversal back up** — a bullish reversal candle off the 20-day EMA, or a close back above a short-term level. The candlestick is the trigger; the moving average is the context.

**Step 4 — Stop.** Place the stop **below the pullback low** (the lowest point of the retracement). If price falls below that low, the pullback was the start of a reversal, not a pause — exit.

**Step 5 — Target.** The natural target is the **prior swing high** (the high before the pullback), or a measured move beyond it. Take profit there; the strategy is about capturing one swing, not riding a trend forever.

## Historical context

"Buy the pullback in an uptrend" is one of the most durable and widely practised techniques in trading, and it is the practical expression of *trend continuation* — the documented tendency of intact trends to resume after pauses. Its logic is the same momentum persistence that underlies the momentum factor, applied to the multi-day timeframe. (It is the mirror image of fading a move: the same pullback that a continuation trader *buys* is what a reversion trader *fades* — the regime call from Chapter 3 decides who is right.)

## A worked example (a liquid NSE stock)

- A stock is in a clear uptrend, above a rising 50-day MA, having climbed from ₹400 to ₹500. The 20-day EMA is at ₹475 and rising.
- The stock **pulls back over a week to ₹478**, touching the rising 20-day EMA. The 50-day MA is still sloping up — uptrend intact.
- A **bullish reversal candle** prints at the 20-day EMA (a long lower shadow — buyers rejected the dip). **Entry** at ₹485.
- **Stop** at ₹468 (just below the pullback low of ₹472). Risk ≈ ₹17.
- The trend resumes and the stock rallies toward the prior high. **Target** at ₹500 (the prior swing high) — a modest reward-to-risk of roughly 0.9:1 by that measure alone (₹15 reward against ₹17 risk).

Result: a defined-risk trade in the direction of the trend, entered at a low-risk point near support. The reward-to-risk here is unremarkable if the trade stops exactly at the prior high — pullback trades are often taken specifically because a strong trend runs *past* the prior swing high before the next stall, which is what improves the realised ratio in practice. A trader who requires a better *stated* R:R before entry should look for a pullback with more room to the prior high, or trail the stop past the first target instead of taking it mechanically.

## Risks and limitations

- **Trend failure.** The defining risk: the "pullback" is actually the start of a reversal. The stop below the pullback low converts this into a small, defined loss — but in a genuine reversal, the loss is taken, not avoided.
- **Missing entries.** In strong trends, price may not reach the 20-day EMA, and the trader waits (or misses the move). This is the cost of demanding a better price.
- **Judgement required.** "Uptrend intact" and "reversal candle" are partly discretionary; the strategy needs written rules to stay consistent.
- **False reversals.** A reversal candle can fail; the stop is the final arbiter, and some failed entries are inevitable.

## Summary

- Buy pullbacks to the rising 20-day EMA within an uptrend (above the rising 50-day MA).
- Enter on a reversal candle; stop below the pullback low; target the prior swing high.
- High win rate, low-risk entry — but requires an existing trend and discipline.
- The mirror of fading; the trend-vs-range call decides which side you are on.
