# 10. Psychology and Common Mistakes

## Why discipline, not intelligence, is the edge

The five strategies in this book are public knowledge. The risk rules are simple arithmetic. If that were enough, everyone who read this far would trade them profitably. Most do not — because momentum trading is *psychologically* brutal, and it attacks the trader in specific, predictable ways. This chapter names the traps and the defences.

## Why momentum trading is psychologically hard

Momentum's payoff profile — **many small losses, a few large winners** — is the exact opposite of what the human brain is wired to enjoy:

- Most trades are **small losses**. Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains feel good (loss aversion), so a strategy that *loses most of the time* feels wrong, even when it is profitable.
- The winners are **few and far between**. Long stretches of losses are interrupted by occasional big wins — and it is precisely during the long losing stretches that most people quit.
- The strategy **demands doing the uncomfortable thing**: buying strength (which feels like chasing) and selling weakness (which feels like locking in a loss).

The result is that momentum trading is *easy to describe and hard to stick to*. The winning trader is not the one with a better strategy; it is the one who can *execute the same strategy through the drawdowns without self-sabotage.*

## The five classic mistakes

### 1. Abandoning the system in a drawdown

After a string of small losses, the natural impulse is to "improve" the strategy — skip a signal, widen a stop, take a smaller position. Each change is an un-tested deviation that destroys the system's edge. *The moment the system is losing is the moment it must be followed most faithfully*, because that is the moment the next big winner is closest.

### 2. Taking profits too early

The whole edge of momentum is the few large winners. Cutting a winner at a small, comfortable profit — "locking it in" — removes exactly the trades that pay for everything else. The discipline is to let the *exit rule* (not the profit) end the trade.

### 3. Moving the stop

A losing trade's stop gets "just a little more room" — then a little more — until a small, planned loss becomes a large, unplanned one. This single habit converts a sound system into a ruinous one. The stop is a commitment made in a calm state; honour it in the emotional state.

### 4. Revenge trading after a stop-out

A stopped-out position is almost always followed by the urge to re-enter immediately, "to get it back." This is how one defined loss becomes an undefined, oversized one. **After a stop, the correct next trade is no trade** — until the plan, not the emotion, says otherwise.

### 5. Overtrading in choppy markets

When the market is range-bound, momentum whipsaws, and the trader responds by trading *more*, chasing every failed breakout. The right response to a trendless market is to trade *less* (or not at all). Activity is not productivity.

## The practices that work

The psychological toolkit is unglamorous but effective:

1. **Write the rules down, and follow them.** A written system is a contract with your future, emotional self. "If price closes above the 20-day high, I enter; if it closes below the 10-day low, I exit" — written, not remembered.
2. **Decide exits at entry.** The stop and the profit-exit are part of the trade's definition, set before the position exists, when judgment is clean.
3. **Judge over many trades, not one.** No single trade proves anything. Momentum is a *distribution* game: it wins over 50 or 100 trades, not on any individual one. Evaluate yourself on the *system's* performance, not the last trade's.
4. **Track process, not P&L.** Review each trade for *whether you followed the rules* — not whether it won. A losing trade that followed the plan is a process win; a winning trade that broke the rules is a future disaster.
5. **Accept the drawdown as the cost.** Momentum's drawdowns are not a bug; they are the premium you pay for the big winners. A trader who accepts this can hold through the pain; one who fights it will abandon the system at the bottom.

## The honest truth

Every failure mode in this chapter is a way of *abandoning your own plan at the worst moment*. Momentum's edge is real but small, and it is expressed only over many disciplined trades. The trader who can simply **do what they said they would do** — take every signal, cut every loss, let every winner run — has an edge that no sophistication can replace. Everything else is detail.

## Summary

- Momentum's payoff profile (many small losses, few big winners) fights human psychology.
- The traps: abandoning the system, cutting winners, moving stops, revenge trading, overtrading.
- The defences: written rules, exits set at entry, long-term judgment, process tracking, accepting drawdowns.
- Discipline — not a better strategy — is the actual edge.
