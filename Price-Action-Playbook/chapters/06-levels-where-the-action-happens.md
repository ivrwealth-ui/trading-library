# 6. Levels: Where the Action Happens

Every setup in this book stands on a level. A level is a price where meaningful two-sided trade has occurred — a place with memory.

## The types of levels that matter

- **Swing highs/lows** — the structure points from Chapter 2.
- **Supply and demand zones** — the origin of sharp moves. A demand zone is the base of a rally that left quickly (unfilled buy orders remain); a supply zone is the origin of a drop.
- **Opening range / previous day's high and low** — the session's value references.
- **Round numbers and big-figure levels** (e.g. Nifty 22,000 / 22,500 — illustrative) — psychological magnets where orders cluster.
- **Volume-at-price peaks (POC)** — the auction's centre of gravity.

## What makes a level *good*

Not all levels are equal. A high-quality level has several of these traits:

1. **It was tested and held** — the more times a level has been respected, the stronger it is (though each test also *weakens* it slightly; see below).
2. **It produced a sharp, decisive move away** — a strong reaction means strong participation.
3. **It coincides** with another reference — a swing low that is also a round number and the previous day's low is a much stronger level than any one of those alone (**confluence**).
4. **It has room** — a level with clear space to run into the next reference is more tradeable than one sandwiched between others.

## Tests and their cost

Every test of a level does two things: it **confirms** the level (more memory) and it **consumes** the level (fewer unfilled orders remain behind it). This is why a level that has been tested five times is both more "known" and more likely to eventually break — the orders that made it hold are being used up.

**How to read:** Trade the *first* test of a strong level with more conviction than the fifth. By the fifth test, be alert for the break rather than the hold.
