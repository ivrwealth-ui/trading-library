# 8. Trend Continuation and Reversal Setups

Beyond failed breakouts, the workhorse setups of price action are the **continuation** trades in a trend and the **reversal** trades at exhaustion.

## Continuation: the pullback and the break-and-retest

In an established trend, the highest-probability trade is usually *with* the trend, entered on a pullback.

- **The pullback (flag).** Price trends, then pulls back on declining momentum, often to a prior swing level or the value area, and resumes. The entry is the *resumption*, not the guessing of the bottom.
- **The break-and-retest.** Price breaks a level and continues, then returns to *retest* the broken level from the other side (former resistance becoming support, or vice versa). A clean retest that holds is a high-quality continuation entry.

**How to read a pullback:** a *healthy* pullback is shallow (holding the prior swing low in an uptrend), on lighter momentum, and time-wise shorter than the impulse that preceded it. A deep, sharp pullback that breaks structure is no longer a pullback — it is a possible reversal.

## Reversal: exhaustion and the two-bar reversal

Reversals are harder and should be taken with tighter criteria. The best reversal setups occur at **extremes with context**:

- **Exhaustion.** A trend that has become vertical, with widening ranges and a final "blow-off" bar, often climaxes. The tell is a sharp rejection bar (a long wick) at a level.
- **The two-bar reversal / engulfing context.** A strong opposite bar that erases the prior bar's range, *at a level*, signals a shift. On its own in the middle of nowhere, an engulfing bar means little; at a confluence of levels, it means a lot.

**The rule that saves traders from bad reversals:** do not short strength or buy weakness *into a trend* — wait for the market to first show you a structure break or a failed breakout (Chapter 7). Reversal trading without that evidence is just fighting the tape.
